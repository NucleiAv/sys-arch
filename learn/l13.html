Message queues, pub sub and event streams

Not every job must finish before you answer the user. Queues let you hand work off, smooth out spikes and let parts of a system fail without taking down the rest.

The idea in one minuteWatch it, step by stepWords you needHow it worksReal life scenariosTrade-offsCommon mistakesSecurity lensPractice. Check yourselfPractice. Interview questionsPractice deskGo deeper

The idea in one minute

When you order food, the app says "order placed" in a second. But the restaurant still has to accept it, a rider must be found, and you need a text message. None of that should make you wait. The app puts a message on a queue and moves on. Workers pick up messages and do the slow jobs in the background.

This is called asynchronous processing. It brings three gifts. Users wait less. Spikes get absorbed, since the queue just grows a little and workers catch up. And if the SMS service is down, orders still work, the messages wait in the queue.

Think of it like this

A restaurant waiter does not cook your meal while you watch. They pin your order on the kitchen rail and go take the next order. Cooks take tickets from the rail at their own pace. The rail is the queue.

Watch it, step by step

Press Next or Play. Each step lights up one part and moves the requests. Arrow keys move steps after you click the diagram.

Words you need

Producer
The part that sends messages.
Consumer
The part that receives and processes them. Also called a worker.
Message queue
A buffer where each message is handled by one consumer. RabbitMQ, Amazon SQS.
Pub sub
Publish subscribe. Each message goes to every interested subscriber group.
Event stream
An append-only log of events that consumers read at their own position and can replay. Kafka.
Acknowledge
The consumer tells the queue it finished, so the message can be removed.
At least once
Every message is delivered, but maybe more than once. The common guarantee.
Dead letter queue
Where messages go after failing too many times.
Backpressure
Slowing producers down when consumers cannot keep up.

How it works

Queue, pub sub or stream

KindWho gets a messageKept after reading?Example

How Kafka style streams scale

A topic is split into partitions. Each partition is an ordered log. Messages with the same key, like the same order id, always go to the same partition, so their order is kept. Consumers in a group share the partitions, so more partitions means more parallel consumers. Since the log is kept, a new service can read history from the start.

Delivery guarantees

  • At most once. Might lose messages. Rarely what you want.
  • At least once. Never lost, maybe duplicated. The usual choice.
  • Exactly once. Possible inside some systems with care, but end to end it usually means at least once plus idempotent consumers, which remember what they already processed.
Key point

Assume every message can arrive twice. Make consumers idempotent, for example by checking "did I already send the SMS for order 81?" before sending.

Ordering

Global order across a whole busy system is expensive. Order per key, per order or per user, is cheap and usually enough.

Real life scenarios

LinkedIn built Kafka

LinkedIn needed to move huge streams of activity events to many systems. They built Kafka as a durable, replayable log and open sourced it. It now powers event pipelines at thousands of companies.

Duplicate welcome emails

A queue redelivered messages after a worker crashed mid task. Users got two welcome emails. Storing processed message ids and skipping repeats fixed it.

A queue saves a flash sale

Orders spiked 30 times for five minutes. The order service just wrote to a queue, and payment workers drained it over 15 minutes. Nothing crashed. Customers got confirmations a few minutes late.

Trade-offs

ChoiceGood atCosts you
Synchronous callSimple, immediate answer and errors.Caller waits, and fails when the other side fails.
QueueAbsorbs spikes, isolates failures, retries.Eventual results, more moving parts, harder debugging.
Work queueEasy scaling of workers.Messages are gone after processing, no replay.
Event streamReplay, many independent consumers, ordered per key.More to run and tune, partitions limit parallelism.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming a message is delivered exactly once.
  • No dead letter queue, so one bad message blocks everything.
  • Putting a queue where the user really needs an immediate answer, like a password check.
  • Not watching queue length. A growing queue is an early warning.
  • Needing a strict global order when per key order would do.

Security lens

Queues move data between services, so they are trust boundaries too.

  • Authenticate producers and consumers. Only the order service may publish order events.
  • Validate messages in consumers. A message on the queue is still untrusted input.
  • Encrypt sensitive fields, since messages may stay on disk for days in a stream.
  • Poison messages can be a denial of service. Dead letter queues keep one bad message from blocking all.
  • Do not put secrets or full card numbers in events.
