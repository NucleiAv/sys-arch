Laws, regulations, governance and compliance

Security is not only technical. Laws set minimum duties, governance decides who is responsible, and compliance proves you did what you promised. Learn how requirements connect to law, what GRC means, and the rules you will meet most often.

The idea in one minute

GRC stands for governance, risk and compliance.

  • Governance is how an organization is directed. Who decides, who is accountable, and which rules everyone follows. Leaders set the direction with policies.
  • Risk management is what you learned in lesson 6.7. Find, score and treat risks.
  • Compliance means meeting the rules you must follow, laws, contracts and standards, and being able to prove it with evidence.

Laws and security requirements are linked. A law says what outcome is needed, for example "protect health data". Your requirements turn that into concrete, testable controls, for example "encrypt health data at rest with AES-256 and log every access".

Key point

Compliance is not the same as security. A company can pass an audit and still be breached. Compliance is the floor, not the ceiling. Use it to get budget and structure, then aim for real risk reduction.

Think of it like this

School rules say "no running in the halls". That is a policy, part of governance. A teacher who watches the halls checks compliance. The school nurse thinks about which kids might fall and where to put soft mats. That is risk management. All three keep kids safe together.

Words you need

Governance
How an organization is directed and held accountable. Roles, decision rights and policies.
Compliance
Meeting required rules and being able to prove it.
Policy
A high level statement of management intent. Mandatory.
Standard
Mandatory specific rules that support a policy, like approved algorithms.
Procedure
Step by step instructions to do a task.
Guideline
Recommended advice. Not mandatory.
Audit
An independent check of controls against requirements.
Evidence
Records that prove a control exists and works.
Data controller
Under GDPR, the organization that decides why and how personal data is used.
Data processor
An organization that handles personal data for a controller.

How it works

The policy pyramid

LevelMandatory?ChangesExample

Who does what, the three lines

  1. First line. Teams that own the work and its risks, like engineering and operations.
  2. Second line. Risk and compliance functions that set rules and watch.
  3. Third line. Internal audit, which checks independently and reports to the board.

Above them, the board is accountable, and a CISO leads security.

Back to the incubator

The incubator touches several rules at once. Device law for the product, health privacy law for the data it sends, and the hospital's own contracts and policies. The architect keeps one requirements to law map, so one control, like encryption, can satisfy several rules at once.

Interview tip

Map controls once, prove many times. A single control set mapped to ISO 27001, SOC 2 and HIPAA saves huge amounts of audit work.

Real life scenarios

British Airways, GDPR fine

Attackers changed a script on the airline's booking site in 2018 and copied card details of hundreds of thousands of customers. The UK regulator fined the company 20 million pounds in 2020, citing weak security measures it should have had.

Anthem, HIPAA settlement

After a 2015 breach of records on about 79 million people, the health insurer paid 16 million dollars in a 2018 settlement with the US health regulator. It was the largest HIPAA settlement at the time.

Uber and a hidden breach

Uber paid hackers in 2016 and called it a bug bounty, instead of reporting the breach. A court later convicted its former security chief over the cover up. Governance failures can become personal, legal problems.

Trade-offs

ChoiceGood atCosts you
Compliance firstClear checklist, audit ready.Can miss real risks the checklist does not name.
Risk firstFocus on real threats.Harder to explain to auditors without a framework.
One control set mapped to many rulesLess duplicate work.Needs a good mapping tool and upkeep.
Many separate programsEach team owns one rule.Duplicate evidence, gaps between programs.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking a passed audit means you are secure.
  • Policies that nobody reads and nobody follows.
  • Collecting evidence by hand, once a year, in a panic.
  • Not knowing which laws apply until a breach happens.
  • Hiding or delaying a breach report.
