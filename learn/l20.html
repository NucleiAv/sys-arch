Monoliths, microservices and API gateways

Should your app be one program or many small ones talking over the network? The honest answer depends on your team more than your traffic. Learn the trade and the tools around it.

The idea in one minuteWatch it, step by stepWords you needHow it worksReal life scenariosTrade-offsCommon mistakesSecurity lensPractice. Check yourselfPractice. Interview questionsPractice deskGo deeper

The idea in one minute

A monolith is one program that does everything, deployed as one unit. A microservices architecture splits the app into many small services, each owning one business area and its own data, talking over the network.

Microservices let many teams work and deploy on their own, and let each part scale and fail separately. But they turn simple function calls into network calls that can be slow or fail, and they need much more tooling to run.

Think of it like this

A monolith is one big kitchen where all cooks share space. Easy to talk, but they bump into each other when the team grows. Microservices are a food court. Each stall runs on its own and can change its menu any time, but now you need a map, signs, and a way to pay at every stall.

Press Next or Play. Each step lights up one part and moves the requests. Arrow keys move steps after you click the diagram.

Words you need

Monolith
One deployable program containing all features.
Modular monolith
One program, but with strict internal modules and boundaries. A great middle ground.
Microservice
A small service that owns one business capability and its data, deployed on its own.
API gateway
The single entry point that handles routing, auth, rate limits and sometimes merging responses.
Backend for frontend
A gateway layer tailored to one client, like mobile or web.
Service discovery
How services find each other's current addresses.
Service mesh
A layer of small proxies beside each service that handle mTLS, retries and metrics. Istio, Linkerd.
Bounded context
A clear business area with its own words and data, a good boundary for a service.
Strangler fig
Slowly replacing parts of an old system by routing feature after feature to new code.

How it works

Monolith versus microservices

MonolithMicroservices
Start upFast and simpleSlow, needs platform work
DeploysAll or nothingEach service on its own
TeamsGreat for one or a few teamsGreat for many teams
Calls between partsIn memory, fast, reliableNetwork, slower, can fail
DataOne database, joins easyEach service owns its data, joins hard
DebuggingOne processNeeds tracing across services
ScalingWhole app togetherEach part separately

Where to draw the lines

Split by business capability, not by technical layer. Good, orders, payments, catalog. Bad, a database service, a validation service. Each service should own its data. If two services must change together for every feature, they should probably be one.

The supporting tools

  • API gateway for one front door.
  • Service discovery, often built into Kubernetes DNS.
  • Event bus or stream for loose coupling, lesson 3.2.
  • Distributed tracing, lesson 4.2.
  • Service mesh when you have many services and want mTLS, retries and metrics without code in every service.

Moving from a monolith

Do not rewrite everything at once. Use the strangler fig approach. Put a gateway in front, carve out one area with clear boundaries, route its traffic to the new service, and repeat.

Key point

Start with a well structured monolith. Split out a service when there is a real reason, a team that needs to move on its own, a part with very different scale, or a part with different reliability or security needs.

Real life scenarios

Amazon's two pizza teams

Amazon split its monolith into services owned by small teams so each team could build and deploy on its own. The motivation was team speed, more than traffic.

Shopify's modular monolith

Shopify kept one huge Ruby on Rails application but enforced strict module boundaries inside it. They got many of the benefits of separation without network calls between parts.

Prime Video went back

An Amazon Prime Video monitoring tool was rebuilt from serverless microservices into a single process and cut its cost by about 90 percent, because the parts passed huge amounts of data between them.

Trade-offs

ChoiceGood atCosts you
MonolithSimple to build, test, deploy and debug.Teams step on each other as the code and team grow.
Modular monolithClear boundaries, still one deploy.Needs discipline to keep modules apart.
MicroservicesIndependent teams, deploys and scaling.Network failures, data consistency, heavy tooling.
Service meshSecurity and resilience without code changes.Another complex layer to run.

Common mistakes

  • Starting a new product with dozens of microservices and a tiny team.
  • Splitting by technical layer instead of business area.
  • Sharing one database between many services, which couples them tightly.
  • Long chains of synchronous calls, where any failure breaks the request.
  • Microservices without tracing, so nobody can debug anything.

Security lens

Microservices add many internal boundaries.

  • Use mutual TLS and service identity, often with a service mesh, so services prove who they are.
  • Check authorization in each service, not only at the gateway.
  • Give each service its own credentials and its own data access.
  • The API gateway is a strong control point. Authentication, rate limits, schema checks and logging.
  • More services means a larger supply chain. Keep an SBOM per service.
