How a card payment works, authorize, capture, refund

A card payment is not one step. The bank first holds the money, then the shop takes it later, and news arrives by webhook. Learn the life of a payment, what can go wrong at each step, and how a payment gateway hides the hard parts.

The idea in one minute

When you pay for shoes online, several companies take part.

  • The merchant, the shop.
  • The payment gateway or processor, such as Stripe, Adyen or Razorpay, which talks to the card networks for the shop.
  • The card network, such as Visa, Mastercard or RuPay.
  • The issuer, your bank, which gave you the card.
  • The acquirer, the shop's bank, which receives the money.

A card payment usually has two main steps.

  1. Authorize. The issuer checks the card and puts a hold on the amount. You see it as pending. No money has moved yet.
  2. Capture. The shop says "I have shipped, take the money". The held amount is then settled to the shop, usually in a batch a day or two later.

Before capture, the shop can void the hold. After capture, it can refund. If a customer disputes the payment with their bank, the bank can force a chargeback.

Think of it like this

At a hotel, they swipe your card at check in to hold some money, in case you empty the minibar. At check out they take only what you owe and let the rest of the hold go. That is authorize, then capture.

Words you need

Issuer
The bank that gave the customer the card.
Acquirer
The bank that receives card payments for the merchant.
Authorization
The issuer approves an amount and places a hold.
Capture
The merchant asks to take an authorized amount.
Void
Cancelling an authorization before capture, which releases the hold.
Settlement
The actual transfer of money between banks, usually in daily batches.
Chargeback
A payment reversed by the customer's bank after a dispute.
Tokenization
Replacing the card number with a token that is useless to thieves.
3-D Secure
An extra check with the issuer, such as a one time password, before authorization.
Webhook
An HTTP call from the gateway to your server when something happens.

Holds do not last forever. Many authorizations expire after about 7 days, but the limit depends on the card network, the card type and the kind of merchant. If you capture after the hold expires, the capture can fail. Shops that ship late either capture early or authorize again.

Webhooks are the source of truth for late events

Some results arrive later, such as a 3-D Secure check, a capture in a batch or a dispute. Gateways send webhooks for these. Rules.

  1. Verify the signature on every webhook, so nobody can fake "payment succeeded".
  2. Reply 200 fast, then process the event from a queue.
  3. Expect duplicates and odd order. Store the event id with a unique rule and skip repeats. Use the payment's latest state from the gateway when in doubt.
  4. Reconcile. Do not rely only on webhooks, see lesson 7.5.

Make every call idempotent

Send an idempotency key with every create, capture and refund call, as in lesson 3.7. A retried capture must never take money twice.

Keep card numbers out of your systems

PCI DSS sets rules for anyone who stores, processes or sends card data. Using the gateway's hosted fields or payment page means the card number goes straight to the gateway, and your servers only see a token. That shrinks your PCI scope a lot.

Key point

Never mark an order paid based on the browser saying "success". Confirm the payment state with the gateway, through its API or a verified webhook.

Real life scenarios

The hold that ran out

A furniture shop authorized cards at checkout and captured when the sofa shipped, often three weeks later. Many captures failed because the holds had expired, and some customers no longer had the money. The shop moved to capturing at order time and refunding if an item could not be delivered.

Fake success page

A small shop marked orders paid when the browser reached the thank you URL. Someone visited the URL directly and got free goods. Checking the payment state with the gateway closed the hole.

The webhook outage

A shop's webhook endpoint was down for six hours during a sale. The gateway retried and the events arrived late, in a burst. Because the handler deduplicated by event id and processed from a queue, nothing was lost or doubled.

Common mistakes

  • Trusting the browser redirect instead of the gateway's verified state.
  • Not verifying webhook signatures.
  • Doing slow work inside the webhook request.
  • Retrying capture or refund without an idempotency key.
  • Storing raw card numbers.

Security lens

Payments attract attackers. Verify every webhook signature, use hosted fields so you never touch card numbers, keep API keys in a secrets manager with only the permissions each service needs, and log every state change for audit. Rate limit payment attempts per card and per account to slow card testing attacks, where criminals try many stolen numbers with small charges.
