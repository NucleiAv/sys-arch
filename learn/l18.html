Observability, SLOs and knowing when things break

You cannot fix what you cannot see. Learn the three signals every system needs, how to set reliability goals that make sense, and how to alert without waking people for nothing.

The idea in one minuteWatch it, step by stepWords you needHow it worksReal life scenariosTrade-offsCommon mistakesCheck yourselfInterview questionsPractice deskGo deeper

The idea in one minute

When a user says "it is slow", you need to find out where, fast. Observability means your system tells you what it is doing. It rests on three kinds of signal.

  • Metrics are numbers over time. Requests per second, error rate, p99 latency, CPU.
  • Logs are written records of events. "Order 81 failed, card declined."
  • Traces follow one request across every service it touched, with timing for each hop.

Then you set goals. A service level objective, SLO, says how reliable something should be, like "99.9 percent of checkout requests succeed in under 500 ms over 30 days".

Think of it like this

Metrics are the dashboard in a car, speed and fuel. Logs are the trip diary. Traces are the GPS track of one journey, showing exactly where you got stuck in traffic.

Watch it, step by step

Press Next or Play. Each step lights up one part and moves the requests. Arrow keys move steps after you click the diagram.

Words you need

Metric
A number measured over time, like requests per second.
Log
A written record of one event, best as structured JSON with fields.
Trace
The full path of one request across services, made of spans.
Span
One step inside a trace, with a start time, duration and labels.
SLI
Service level indicator. The measurement, like percent of requests under 300 ms.
SLO
Service level objective. The target for an SLI, like 99.9 percent.
SLA
Service level agreement. A promise to customers with penalties if broken.
Error budget
How much failure the SLO allows. 99.9 percent leaves 0.1 percent to spend.
Golden signals
Latency, traffic, errors and saturation. The four metrics to watch first.

How it works

What to measure first, the golden signals

  1. Latency. How long requests take. Watch p50 and p99, not just the average.
  2. Traffic. How many requests.
  3. Errors. How many fail.
  4. Saturation. How full things are, CPU, memory, queue length, connection pools.

Nines and what they allow

AvailabilityDowntime a yearDowntime a month
99 percentabout 3.65 daysabout 7 hours
99.9 percentabout 8.8 hoursabout 43 minutes
99.99 percentabout 53 minutesabout 4.3 minutes
99.999 percentabout 5 minutesabout 26 seconds

Each extra nine costs a lot more. Users on mobile networks often cannot tell the difference above 99.9.

Error budgets

If the SLO is 99.9 percent, you may fail 0.1 percent of requests in the month. While budget remains, ship features fast. When it is spent, slow down and work on reliability. This turns arguments between speed and stability into a simple number.

Alerts that people trust

  • Alert on symptoms users feel, like the error rate of checkout, not on every high CPU.
  • Use burn rate alerts, page someone when the error budget is being spent too fast.
  • Every alert must be actionable. If the answer is "ignore it", delete the alert.
Key point

Structured logs with a trace id and a request id in every line let you jump from a metric spike to the exact failing requests.

Real life scenarios

Google's SRE book

Google popularized SLOs and error budgets. Teams agree on a target and use the remaining budget to decide whether to launch or to fix reliability.

The alert nobody read

A team had 300 alerts a week. A real outage alert drowned in the noise. They deleted 80 percent, kept symptom based alerts and paged only on SLO burn.

A trace finds a hidden N plus 1

A page made one database query per item in a list, 200 queries for 200 items. Each query was fast, so metrics looked fine, but the trace showed 200 tiny bars adding up to 2 seconds.

Trade-offs

ChoiceGood atCosts you
More loggingMore detail when debugging.Storage cost, noise, risk of logging private data.
Sampling tracesMuch cheaper.May miss the rare slow request, use tail based sampling to keep slow ones.
Tight SLOUsers happier.Expensive, slows feature work.
Loose SLOFaster shipping.Users may leave.

Common mistakes

  • Watching only averages, which hide the slow tail.
  • Alerting on causes like CPU instead of symptoms like errors.
  • Logging passwords, tokens or personal data.
  • Promising customers 99.99 percent in an SLA while the system depends on parts with 99.9.
  • No trace id across services, so logs cannot be joined.
