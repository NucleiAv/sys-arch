Fraud detection, risk scores and the cost of saying no

Every payment system attracts fraud. Block too little and you pay for stolen cards. Block too much and you turn away honest customers. Learn how risk scoring works, how to set block and review lines, and how fraud systems fit into the payment flow.

The idea in one minute

For each payment, a fraud system gives a risk score, for example from 0 to 100. Higher means more likely to be fraud. The score comes from signals, facts about the payment.

  • Is this a new account or a new card?
  • How many payments has this card made in the last hour, called velocity?
  • Does the shipping country match the card's country?
  • Is the device or IP address linked to past fraud?
  • Is the amount much larger than this customer usually spends?

Then two lines turn the score into a decision. Below the review line, approve. Above the block line, decline. In between, send the payment to manual review, or ask for extra proof such as 3-D Secure.

Moving the lines is a trade-off. A low block line catches more fraud but also blocks more honest customers, which are false positives. A high line lets more fraud through, the false negatives. There is no perfect setting, only the right cost balance for your business.

Think of it like this

A shop guard watches the door. If he stops everyone in a hoodie, he catches some thieves but also annoys many good customers. If he stops nobody, thieves walk out. He has to pick which signs are worth a second look.

Words you need

Risk score
A number that estimates how likely a payment is fraud.
Signal
A fact used to score a payment, such as account age or velocity.
Velocity
How many actions happen in a time window, such as payments per card per hour.
False positive
An honest payment that was blocked.
False negative
A fraudulent payment that was approved.
Manual review
A person checks a payment the system was unsure about.
Card testing
Criminals try many stolen card numbers with small payments to find which ones work.
Chargeback rate
Share of payments that end in a dispute. Card networks watch it, and high rates bring fines.

How it works

Rules and models together

RulesMachine learning model
ExampleBlock if more than 5 cards on one device in 10 minutesA model trained on past fraud gives a probability
Good atClear, known attacks, fast to changeSubtle patterns across many signals
WeaknessAttackers learn the rules, many rules conflictNeeds labelled data, harder to explain

Most systems use both. Rules catch obvious attacks and enforce policy. The model handles the grey zone.

Where it sits in the payment flow

The score must be ready before authorization, so it has a tight time budget, often tens of milliseconds. Signals like velocity come from fast counters, for example in Redis, updated as payments arrive. Slower checks can run after approval and before shipping.

Choosing the lines

Think in money, not only in accuracy.

  • Cost of a false negative, the fraud amount plus fees plus the chargeback.
  • Cost of a false positive, the lost sale and an unhappy customer who may not return.
  • Cost of review, staff time and slower delivery.

Move the block and review lines and measure all three. Shops that sell digital goods with instant delivery usually set stricter lines than shops that ship physical goods slowly, because they have no time to review.

Labels arrive late

You only learn that a payment was fraud when a chargeback arrives, weeks later. So model training always looks at the past, and new attack patterns get through at first. Rules let you respond to a new attack the same day.

Key point

Fraud prevention is a cost trade, not a search for zero fraud. Blocking every risky payment can cost more than the fraud itself.

Real life scenarios

Card testing on a donation page

A charity's donation page let anyone pay 1 rupee. Criminals used it to test thousands of stolen cards in a night. Velocity rules per IP and device, a CAPTCHA and a minimum amount stopped it.

Too strict at launch

A new shop blocked every order where the shipping and billing addresses differed. It also blocked gifts, which were a quarter of its orders around festivals. Sending those orders to review instead of blocking won them back.

The slow signal

A fraud model needed a call to a slow external service and sometimes took 2 seconds, so checkouts timed out. Moving the signal to a cache refreshed in the background brought scoring under 50 ms.

Trade-offs

ChoiceGood atCosts you
Strict linesLess fraud loss.More honest customers refused.
Loose linesMore sales, happier customers.More fraud and chargebacks.
Manual reviewGood decisions on hard cases.Staff cost and slower orders.
Step up with 3-D SecureShifts some liability to the issuer and stops many fraudsters.Some honest customers drop out at the extra step.

Common mistakes

  • Measuring only fraud caught, never honest customers blocked.
  • A scorer that is too slow for the checkout.
  • Only machine learning, no rules for fast response.
  • Never updating rules after attackers adapt.
  • Treating review as a black hole with no feedback to the model.

Security lens

Fraud systems hold sensitive data, such as device fingerprints and behaviour, so they fall under privacy law. Tell users how data is used, keep it only as long as needed, and restrict access. Protect the rules too. If attackers learn the exact thresholds, they will stay just below them.
