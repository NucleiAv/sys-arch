Security in the field, patches, updates, intel and incidents

Shipping is the start, not the end. Learn how products stay secure for years with patch management and safe over the air updates, how threat intelligence tells you what to fix first, and how to handle an incident.

The idea in one minute

New vulnerabilities are found every day in code that was fine yesterday. A product that cannot be updated will become unsafe. So the architect must plan four things before launch.

  1. Patch management. How you find, test and ship fixes, and how fast.
  2. Over the air updates, OTA. How fixes reach devices safely, without bricking them.
  3. Threat intelligence. How you learn about new threats that affect your product.
  4. Incident management. What you do when something goes wrong.

For a medical device this is also a legal duty. Regulators expect a plan to monitor, find and fix vulnerabilities after the product is on the market.

Think of it like this

A car needs oil changes, recalls and a number to call after a crash. Software products need the same things. Updates, warnings about new dangers and a plan for bad days.

Words you need

Patch management
The process to find, test, approve and install fixes.
OTA update
Over the air update. Software delivered to devices over a network.
A and B slots
Two copies of the system. Update the spare one and switch, so a failed update can roll back.
Anti rollback
A rule that stops installing an older, vulnerable version, often with a counter in secure storage.
Staged rollout
Releasing to a small group first, then more, watching health at each stage.
Threat intelligence
Information about threats, attackers and vulnerabilities that helps you decide what to do.
CVE
Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures. A public id for one known vulnerability, like CVE-2021-44228.
ISAC
Information Sharing and Analysis Center. A group where companies in one sector share threat information. Health-ISAC for health care.
Incident
An event that harms or threatens the security of a system or its data.
CVD
Coordinated vulnerability disclosure. A process for researchers to report bugs and for the maker to fix them before details go public.

How it works

Patch management for products

  1. Monitor. Watch your SBOM against vulnerability feeds, and take reports from researchers.
  2. Assess. Is the bug in our product? Is it reachable? What harm can it do? Use the risk method from lesson 6.7.
  3. Plan. Fix, or use a compensating control, and tell customers.
  4. Test. Regression, safety and security tests. Medical devices also check if the change needs regulator review.
  5. Release. Staged rollout with monitoring.
  6. Verify. Track how many devices run the fix. Old versions in the field are risk you still own.

Many teams set fix deadlines by severity, for example critical in 15 days, high in 30 days, and faster for bugs on the CISA KEV list.

Safe over the air updates

  • Signed images checked before install and at boot.
  • Anti rollback so an attacker cannot install an old, signed but vulnerable version.
  • A and B slots and a health check, with automatic rollback.
  • Staged rings with a stop button.
  • Safe timing. Never interrupt a clinical function.
  • Update the update system. Frameworks like The Update Framework, TUF, and Uptane for cars protect against a stolen key or a hacked update server.

Incident management

NIST SP 800-61 describes the classic phases.

  1. Preparation. Plans, contacts, logs, tools and practice drills.
  2. Detection and analysis. Notice something, confirm it, judge its scope.
  3. Containment, eradication and recovery. Stop the spread, remove the cause, restore service.
  4. Post incident activity. A blameless review and fixes so it does not happen again.

Revision 3 of the guide, from 2025, maps this work to the six functions of the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0.

Careful

Many laws have short deadlines to report. GDPR gives 72 hours to tell the regulator after you become aware of a personal data breach. Know your deadlines before an incident, not during one.

Real life scenarios

CrowdStrike update, July 2024

A faulty content update for a security product crashed about 8.5 million Windows computers in hours, including hospital and airline systems. It went to all customers at once. Staged rollout and stronger testing of content updates were among the fixes.

Jeep recall by USB stick, 2015

After the remote hack, the car maker could not update cars over the air. It mailed USB sticks to owners and blocked the port on the mobile network. Products that can update safely over the air recover much faster.

Change Healthcare, 2024

Ransomware hit a large US health payment company. Attackers entered through a remote access portal with no MFA. Pharmacies and hospitals across the country could not process claims for weeks, and data on over 100 million people was stolen.

ChoiceGood atCosts you
Fast patchingSmaller window for attackers.Less testing time, risk of breaking things.

Public disclosureCustomers can protect themselves.Attackers learn too. Coordinate timing.

Common mistakes

  • A product with no way to update in the field.
  • Updates without signatures or anti rollback.
  • Pushing an update to every device at once.
  • No SBOM, so you cannot tell if a new CVE affects you.
  • An incident plan that nobody has practiced.
  • No public way for researchers to report bugs.
