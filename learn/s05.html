Threat modeling, step by step

Threat modeling is the habit of asking "what can go wrong?" before attackers do. Learn the four questions, how to draw a data flow diagram, how to use STRIDE, and which other methods and tools exist.

The idea in one minute

A threat model is a short, shared study of a design. Adam Shostack puts it in four questions.

  1. What are we working on? Draw the system.
  2. What can go wrong? List threats.
  3. What are we going to do about it? Pick a response for each threat.
  4. Did we do a good job? Check the model and the fixes.

You do it early, when the design is still a drawing, and again when the design changes. It is a team activity. Developers, testers, product owners and security people each see different threats.

Think of it like this

Before a school trip, the teacher asks "what can go wrong?" A child gets lost, it rains, someone forgets lunch. For each one she plans something. Name tags, raincoats, spare sandwiches. That is a threat model.

Watch it, step by step

Press Next or Play. Each step lights up one part and moves the requests. Arrow keys move steps after you click the diagram.

Words you need

Threat model
A structured look at a design to find what can go wrong and decide what to do about it.
DFD
Data flow diagram. External entities, processes, data stores, data flows and trust boundaries.
STRIDE
Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information disclosure, Denial of service, Elevation of privilege. A checklist of threat types.
Attack tree
A tree with the attacker's goal at the top and the ways to reach it as branches.
PASTA
Process for Attack Simulation and Threat Analysis. A seven stage, risk centric method.
LINDDUN
A privacy threat method. Linking, Identifying, Non-repudiation, Detecting, Data disclosure, Unawareness, Non-compliance.
MITRE ATT&CK
A public knowledge base of real attacker tactics and techniques.
Mitigation
A change that reduces the chance or the harm of a threat.

How it works

The parts of a data flow diagram

ShapeMeaningExample
RectangleExternal entity, outside your controlNurse, hospital records system
CircleProcess, your codeDevice API, update service
Two linesData storeSettings file, log, database
ArrowData flow"set temperature" message
Dashed lineTrust boundaryDevice zone to hospital network

STRIDE, each letter breaks one property

ThreatProperty it breaksTypical fix
SpoofingAuthenticityStrong authentication, certificates
TamperingIntegritySignatures, MACs, TLS, access control on files
RepudiationNon-repudiationSigned audit logs with user ids
Information disclosureConfidentialityEncryption, least data, access control
Denial of serviceAvailabilityRate limits, quotas, isolation, redundancy
Elevation of privilegeAuthorizationRole checks, sandboxing, least privilege

Walk every element and every flow that crosses a boundary, and ask each letter. This is called STRIDE per element.

Four responses to a threat

  1. Mitigate. Add a control.
  2. Eliminate. Remove the feature or the data.
  3. Transfer. Let someone else carry it, like a cloud provider or insurance.
  4. Accept. Record it, with a named owner who agrees.

Other methods and when to use them

MethodFocusUse when
STRIDEThreat types per elementMost software and device designs
Attack treesOne attacker goal in depthA high value target, like "change the dose"
PASTABusiness risk and attack simulationYou need to link threats to business impact
LINDDUNPrivacySystems with personal or health data
ATT&CK and CAPECReal attacker behaviour and attack patternsTesting detection, or making threats concrete
DREADScoring threatsOlder method. Many teams now use CVSS or a risk matrix instead.

Tools

You can threat model on a whiteboard. Tools help keep it up to date. Examples are the Microsoft Threat Modeling Tool, OWASP Threat Dragon, pytm which builds models from Python code, Threagile which uses YAML, and commercial tools like IriusRisk.

Interview tip

A threat model is never done. Update it when you add a feature, a new interface or a new supplier. Keep it short enough that people read it.

Real life scenarios

Microsoft and STRIDE

Microsoft created STRIDE in 1999 and made threat modeling a required step in its Security Development Lifecycle after the worm years of the early 2000s. Threat modeling found design flaws that code scanners never see.

An attack tree on a pump

A team built an attack tree for "give the wrong dose". Branches were the radio, the drug library file, the touch screen and the service port. The drug library file had no signature. It became the top fix, though nobody had thought of it before.

The flow nobody drew

A company modeled its web app but not the nightly export to a partner over plain FTP. The export held customer data. A breach came through that flow. A full DFD would have shown it at once.

Trade-offs

ChoiceGood atCosts you
STRIDE per elementSystematic, easy to teach.Can produce long lists of small threats.
Attack treesDeep look at one goal.Only as complete as the people who build them.
PASTATies threats to business risk.Heavy, takes days of work.
Tool based modelingEasy to update and share.Tools can make people tick boxes instead of thinking.

Common mistakes

  • Threat modeling after the code is finished.
  • A model that only the security team understands.
  • Leaving out flows like backups, exports, logs and support access.
  • Listing threats without a response and an owner.
  • Never updating the model after the design changes.
