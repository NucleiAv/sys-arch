Security controls and defenses

A control is anything that lowers risk. Learn the kinds of controls, how to choose the right ones under real limits of money, time and safety, and what to do when the ideal control is not possible.

The idea in one minute

A control is a safeguard. A lock, a log, a backup, a training course and a firewall rule are all controls. Each one lowers the chance that something bad happens, or lowers the harm when it does.

You sort controls in two ways.

  • By what they do. Stop it, find it, fix it, scare it off, or replace a control you cannot have.
  • By what they are. A rule for people, a piece of technology, or something physical.

You never pick controls from a shopping list alone. You start from a risk, look at your limits, and choose the set that brings the risk down to a level the owner accepts.

Think of it like this

To keep your bike safe you use a lock, that stops theft. You park it where a camera sees it, that finds the thief. You have insurance, that fixes the loss. A sticker saying "alarm fitted" scares thieves away. Together they protect the bike better than one huge lock.

Words you need

Preventive control
Stops an event before it happens. MFA, firewalls, input validation.
Detective control
Finds an event while or after it happens. Logs, alerts, intrusion detection.
Corrective control
Fixes the damage. Patching, restoring from backup, isolating a host.
Deterrent control
Discourages an attacker. Warning banners, visible cameras.
Compensating control
An alternative when the normal control is not possible.
Administrative control
Policies, training, procedures and background checks.
Technical control
Built into technology. Encryption, access control, logging.
Physical control
Locks, badges, guards, tamper seals on devices.
Residual risk
The risk that remains after controls are in place.

How to choose controls

  1. Start from the risk. Which threat, which asset, how bad.
  2. Look at control catalogs. NIST SP 800-53, CIS Critical Security Controls, ISO/IEC 27001 Annex A or IEC 62443 give options.
  3. Check the limits. Cost, time, skills, performance, usability, safety and certification.
  4. Prefer controls that remove whole classes of problems. A memory safe language removes many bugs at once. Signed updates remove all fake update attacks.
  5. Layer them. Prevent, detect and recover, so one failure is not a breach.
  6. Measure. Check that the control works, with tests, logs and audits.

Constraints that change the answer

  • Safety. A screen lock on an incubator must never hide an alarm. Prefer badge tap login and leave alarms always visible.
  • Certification. Changing a medical device can need new regulator review. Some controls go in the network around the device instead.
  • Power and memory. A small device may not run a full antivirus. Use allow listing and secure boot instead.
  • Legacy. Old systems that cannot be patched get compensating controls, like isolation and monitoring.

Advanced principles

  • Defense in depth. Several independent layers.
  • Diversity of defense. Layers from different vendors or methods, so one bug does not beat them all.
  • Zero trust. Check identity and device health on every request.
  • Assume breach. Design as if an attacker is already inside, so detection and containment matter.
Key point

A compensating control must give similar protection to the one it replaces, and it must be written down with the reason. Auditors will ask.

Real life scenarios

WannaCry and the NHS, 2017

Ransomware spread through an old Windows file sharing flaw. A patch had been out for two months. Many UK hospital systems were hit and some appointments and operations were cancelled. Patching is a corrective control, and segmentation would have slowed the spread.

Colonial Pipeline, 2021

Attackers logged in to an old VPN account that had no MFA, using a leaked password. The company shut down fuel pipeline operations for days. One preventive control, MFA on remote access, would have blocked the entry.

A compensating control in a lab

A hospital lab had analysers running an old operating system that the vendor would not let anyone patch. The team put them on their own network, allowed only the lab system to talk to them and alerted on any other traffic.

Trade-offs

ChoiceGood atCosts you
Preventive controlsStop harm before it starts.Can block real users, and none are perfect.
Detective controlsCatch what got through.Need people to watch and act on alerts.

Many layersOne failure is not a breach.Cost and complexity grow with each layer.

Common mistakes

  • Buying a tool before knowing which risk it lowers.
  • Only preventive controls, with no way to detect or recover.
  • Controls that break safety or usability, so staff bypass them.
  • Backups that are online and get encrypted by the same ransomware.
  • Never testing that a control still works.
