CAP, PACELC and how fresh your data really is

When machines cannot talk to each other, you must choose between giving an answer that may be old and giving no answer. Learn the famous rule and what it means in practice.

The idea in one minuteWatch it, step by stepWords you needHow it worksReal life scenariosTrade-offsCommon mistakesSecurity lensPractice. Check yourselfPractice. Interview questionsPractice deskGo deeper

The idea in one minute

With data on many machines, networks will sometimes split them into groups that cannot talk. This is a network partition. It is not rare. Cables get cut, switches fail, cloud zones lose links.

During a partition, a machine that gets a request has two options. Answer with what it has, which might be out of date, or refuse until it can check with the others. That is the CAP theorem. During a Partition you must pick Consistency, every read sees the latest write, or Availability, every request gets an answer.

Think of it like this

Two shop branches share one stock list by phone. The phone line goes dead. A customer asks for the last blue jacket. Branch A can sell it and risk that branch B also sells it, available. Or it can say "please wait until the line is back", consistent. It cannot be both.

Words you need

Partition
A network break that splits machines into groups that cannot talk.
Consistency in CAP
Every read sees the most recent write, as if there were one copy. Also called linearizability.
Availability in CAP
Every request to a working node gets an answer, even if not the newest.
Strong consistency
Reads always return the latest write.
Eventual consistency
If writes stop, all copies become the same eventually. Until then reads may be stale.
Read your writes
You always see your own changes, even if others may see them later.
Monotonic reads
Once you have seen a value, you never see an older one.
PACELC
If Partition, choose A or C. Else, choose Latency or Consistency.

How it works

The part people forget, PACELC

Partitions are rare. Most of the time the network is fine, and you still face a choice. To be strongly consistent, a write must wait for other copies to confirm, which takes time. So Else, when there is no partition, you trade Latency against Consistency. That is PACELC.

System styleDuring a partitionNormal times
Cassandra, DynamoDB defaultStays availableFast, eventually consistent
Single leader SQL with sync replicasRefuses writes on the minority sideConsistent, slower writes
Spanner, CockroachDBConsistentConsistent, pays latency

A ladder of consistency

From strongest to weakest. Linearizable, one copy illusion. Sequential and causal, everyone sees changes in an order that respects cause and effect, so a reply never shows before its question. Read your writes and monotonic reads, guarantees per user. Eventual, copies agree sometime.

Choosing per feature, not per company

FeatureWhat it needs
Bank balance, ticket seat, unique usernameStrong
Shopping cart, chat deliveryRead your writes, causal order
Like counts, view counts, follower countsEventual is fine
Search index, recommendationsEventual, minutes is fine
Key point

Saying "it depends on the feature" and then choosing per feature is the mature answer. One system can use strong consistency for payments and eventual for likes.

Real life scenarios

Amazon's shopping cart chose availability

The Dynamo paper describes why Amazon kept carts always writable. If two copies disagree, the carts are merged, so an item might come back after you removed it. A slightly wrong cart is better than a cart that does not work during a sale.

Bank transfers choose consistency

A bank would rather show 'service unavailable' than let two ATMs on two sides of a partition both pay out the same balance.

Google Spanner uses clocks to be consistent and global

Spanner uses atomic clocks and GPS to know time tightly and gives strong consistency across continents, paying with a little extra latency on writes.

Trade-offs

ChoiceGood atCosts you
Strong consistencySimple to reason about, never stale.Slower writes, may refuse requests during partitions.
Eventual consistencyFast and always available.Stale reads and conflicts the app must handle.
Per user guaranteesFeels right to each user, cheap.Different users may briefly see different things.

Common mistakes

  • Saying "I pick CA". You cannot opt out of partitions in a distributed system.
  • Using eventual consistency for things like money or inventory at checkout.
  • Using strong consistency everywhere and paying latency for like counts.
  • Thinking eventual means "maybe never". With healthy systems it is usually milliseconds to seconds.

Security lens

Consistency choices affect security decisions.

  • A permission change that is eventually consistent may let a removed user act for a few seconds. For access removal, read from the leader or use short token lifetimes.
  • Revocation lists and deny lists must be read fresh, not from an old cache.
  • Audit logs need strong ordering and integrity. Append only storage helps.
