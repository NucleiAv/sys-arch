Back of the envelope math

Rough math that fits on a napkin tells you if you need one server or a thousand. Learn the handful of numbers and tricks that every architect uses.

The idea in one minute

Before you draw boxes, you need to know how big the problem is. You do not need exact numbers. You need the right size. Is it 10 requests a second, or 10 thousand, or 10 million? Each size needs a very different design.

Think of it like this

If you are planning a party, you do not need to know that exactly 47 people will come. You need to know if it is about 5, about 50 or about 500. That decides if you cook at home, rent a hall or hire a caterer.

The trick is to round hard. A day has 86,400 seconds. Call it 100 thousand. Now 10 million requests a day is about 100 a second. Easy.

Watch it, step by step

Press Next or Play. Each step lights up one part and moves the requests. Arrow keys move steps after you click the diagram.

Words you need

DAU
Daily active users. The usual starting number.
QPS or RPS
Queries or requests per second.
Peak factor
How much busier the busiest time is than the average. Usually 2 to 5.
Read to write ratio
How many reads for each write. Social apps are often 100 to 1 or more.
Order of magnitude
The size in powers of ten. 3,000 and 7,000 are the same order, about thousands.

How it works

Numbers to remember

ThingRough size
Seconds in a dayabout 100 thousand (86,400)
Seconds in a monthabout 2.5 million
1 thousand, million, billion bytesKB, MB, GB, then TB, PB
A short text postabout 1 KB
A photoabout 200 KB to 2 MB
A minute of HD videoabout 50 MB

How long things take

ActionRough time
Read from memory100 nanoseconds
Read from a fast SSD100 microseconds
Trip inside one data center0.5 ms
Read from a spinning disk10 ms
Trip across a continent50 to 100 ms
Trip around the world150 to 250 ms
Key point

Memory is about 1,000 times faster than SSD, and the network across the world is about 1,000 times slower than inside a data center. That is why caches and nearby servers matter.

What one machine can do, roughly

PartRough capacity
One web app server1 to 10 thousand simple requests a second
One SQL databasea few thousand writes and tens of thousands of simple reads a second
One Redis cache100 thousand or more operations a second
One Kafka partitiontens of MB a second

These depend on hardware and work done. In an interview, say your guess and move on.

The four step recipe

  1. Users a day times actions each gives actions a day.
  2. Divide by 100 thousand to get per second.
  3. Multiply by 2 to 5 for the peak.
  4. Actions a day times size times 365 gives storage a year. Add copies, usually times 3.

Real life scenarios

A startup buys 50 servers it does not need

They guessed traffic from their dream number of users, not real numbers. A day of real traffic was 200 thousand requests, about 2 a second. One small server would do. Estimates protect money as well as uptime.

A ticket site crashes at 10 AM

Average traffic was tiny, so they sized for the average. But every fan arrived at 10:00 sharp, about 1,000 times the average. Always ask, what is the peak, and when?

Picking the storage for a chat app

500 million messages a day at 100 bytes is 50 GB a day, about 18 TB a year, times 3 copies is over 50 TB. That is too big for one SQL database, which tells you early to plan for a database that spreads data across machines.

Trade-offs

ChoiceGood atCosts you
Round hardFast, easy to say out loud, gets the right size.Off by maybe 30 percent. That is fine.
Exact mathPrecise.Slow, easy to make mistakes under pressure, adds nothing to the design.
Plan for average loadCheaper.Falls over at the busy hour.
Plan for peak with headroomSurvives spikes.Some machines sit idle at night. Autoscaling helps.

Common mistakes

  • Doing long division in an interview. Round to powers of ten.
  • Forgetting the peak. The average is not the problem.
  • Forgetting replicas and backups when sizing storage.
  • Mixing bits and bytes. Network speed is often in bits, 8 bits make a byte.
  • Spending 15 minutes on math. Five is plenty.

Security lens

Estimates help security too.

  • Size your logs. Audit logs for 1 million users with 50 events a day each is 50 million lines a day. Plan storage and retention.
  • Size attack traffic. A small botnet can send hundreds of thousands of requests a second. Your edge and rate limits must hold far more than normal peak.
  • Estimate password guessing. At 10 guesses a second per IP with 1000 IPs, a 6 digit PIN falls in under two minutes. That is why lockouts and MFA matter.
