Design a shopping cart and checkout

A cart looks simple, a list of items. But it must survive a phone switch, a closed browser and a long weekend, merge when a guest logs in, and at checkout it must check that prices and stock are still true. Design a cart that never loses items and never sells at a wrong price.

The ask

Shoppers add items to a cart from the web or the app, logged in or not. The cart should follow them across devices once they log in, last for weeks, and lead to a checkout that charges the right amount for items that are really in stock.

Pin down the requirements

What it must do

  • Add, remove and change quantities, as a guest or a logged in user.
  • Keep the cart across devices and for at least 30 days.
  • Merge a guest cart into the user's cart on login.
  • At checkout, recheck price, stock and promotions, then place the order.

How well it must do it

  • Adding to the cart takes under 100 ms.
  • The cart is always available, even if parts of the catalog are slow.
  • Checkout totals are always correct.

Out of scope, say it out loud

  • Search, recommendations, payment details, see the payment designs.

Do the math

20 million daily shoppers. Each changes their cart 10 times a day. 5 percent check out. A cart row is about 2 KB.

Key point

Cart changes are many and simple. Checkout is rare and strict. Design them differently.

The API

GET    /v1/cart                         cart for the session or user
PUT    /v1/cart/items/{sku}             { "quantity": 2 }     set, not add, so retries are safe
DELETE /v1/cart/items/{sku}
POST   /v1/cart/merge                   on login, guest cart id from the cookie
POST   /v1/checkout                     { "cart_version": 14, "idempotency_key": "..." }
     -> { order_id, total } or { changes: [price changed, out of stock] }

The data model

A key value or document store fits carts well, one document per cart, read and written whole. The price stored in the cart is only for display. The real price is looked up again at checkout.

The big picture

  1. Cart service with a durable store, such as DynamoDB, Cassandra or a sharded SQL table. A cache in front is optional, but the store is the truth.
  2. Guest carts keyed by a random id in a cookie, with a 30 day expiry.
  3. Merge on login with clear rules. Union of items, keep the larger quantity, drop unavailable items.
  4. Checkout service that recalculates everything from sources of truth, the catalog for prices, inventory for stock, promotions for discounts, and taxes.
  5. Order saga, lesson 7.4. Hold stock, charge payment, create the order, with compensations.
  6. Optimistic version on the cart, so a checkout started from an old view of the cart is caught.

Deep dives

Where to keep carts

Browser storage alone loses the cart when the shopper switches device. A cache with a short TTL loses carts over a long weekend. A durable store keeps them for weeks and across devices. Many shops keep a copy in the browser for speed and the durable store as the truth.

Merging carts

When a guest logs in, both a guest cart and a saved cart may exist. A simple rule is the union of items, with the larger quantity when both have the same item. Tell the shopper what changed.

Amazon's Dynamo

Amazon's 2007 Dynamo paper used the shopping cart as its main example. Dynamo chose to keep accepting add to cart writes even during failures, and merge different versions later. The cost was that a deleted item could sometimes reappear. For a cart, a lost add was judged worse than a returning item.

Price changes and promotions

Store the price seen when added only for display. At checkout, look up the current price and promotion rules, and show any difference before charging. Never charge a price the shopper has not seen.

Abandoned carts

Most carts never reach checkout. They are useful for reminder emails, but respect consent and email rules, and delete expired guest carts.

Trade-offs you must name

ChoiceGood atCosts you
Durable store for cartsSurvives devices and time.More writes to a database.

Security and abuse

Never trust prices or totals sent by the client. Recompute them on the server at checkout, or a shopper can edit a request and buy a TV for 1 rupee. Make guest cart ids long and random, so nobody can guess another shopper's cart. Limit quantities per order for scarce items to slow down bots and resellers.
