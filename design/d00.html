The method, how to design anything

One simple order of steps that works for every design, from a school project to a billion users. Learn it once, then use it on every task in this track.

Why you need a method

A design question is open on purpose. "Design a chat app" could mean a two person toy or a system for two billion people. If you start drawing at once, you will solve the wrong problem.

So every good architect follows the same order. First understand the problem, then size it, then draw it, then stress it. The order matters more than any single box.

Think of it like this

Before you build a treehouse you ask who it is for, how many kids will climb in at once and how high it is. A treehouse for one child and a treehouse for a whole class need very different wood.

Watch it work

Press Next or Play. Each step lights up one part and moves the requests. Arrow keys move steps after you click the diagram.

The six steps, in detail

1. Requirements, 5 minutes

Ask. Do not assume. Split what you hear into two lists.

  • Functional. What can a user do? "Send a message. See if it was read."
  • Non-functional. How well? "Messages arrive in under one second. Never lose a message. 99.99 percent uptime."

Then say what you will not do. "I will skip voice calls and group admin features."

2. Estimates, 5 minutes

Turn users into load. Daily active users, then requests per second, then storage per year. Round hard. You want the size, not the exact number. Lesson 1.8 teaches the tricks.

3. API, 5 minutes

Write the 3 to 5 calls the client needs. POST /messages, GET /messages?chat=123&before=.... This turns fuzzy features into exact inputs and outputs.

4. Data model, 5 minutes

Write the main records and their keys. Then ask, "how do I look these up?". The lookup shape picks the database, not the other way round.

5. High level design, 10 minutes

Draw the simplest boxes and arrows that do the job. Client, load balancer, service, database, cache. Walk one request through it out loud.

6. Deep dives, 15 minutes

Find the hardest part. Usually the busiest path, the biggest data or the trickiest correctness rule. Scale it, make it survive failure, and say what you traded.

Moves that make you look senior

Lead with numbers

Say "that is about 1,200 writes a second, which one Postgres can handle". A number turns an opinion into a decision.

Name every trade-off

Every time you pick something, say what you gave up. "I pick a cache, which means a user may see a list that is a second old."

Start simple, then grow

Draw the version for today, then say what breaks first at 10 times and 100 times the load. Never start with 40 microservices.

Check in with the interviewer

Ask "should I go deeper on storage or on delivery?". It shows you can work with people and it uses your time well.

Design for failure

For every box ask what happens if it dies. Then fix the answers that are "everything stops".

ChoiceGood atCosts you
Draw first, ask laterFeels fast.You solve the wrong problem and waste the interview.

Security and abuse

Add one more step to the method. After the deep dives, ask what can go wrong on purpose. Who would attack this, what do they want, and where are the trust boundaries? Name two or three threats and the controls for them. Lesson 6.5 shows how.
