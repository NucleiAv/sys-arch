Design a ride sharing app

A rider taps "request" and within seconds a nearby driver is matched, both see each other moving on the map, and the trip is priced. Like Uber or Ola.

The ask

Riders request trips. The system finds nearby available drivers, offers the trip, tracks both on a live map, and handles the trip from pickup to payment.

Pin down the requirements

What it must do

  • Drivers send their location every few seconds while online.
  • Riders see nearby cars and request a ride.
  • Match a rider to a nearby driver who accepts.
  • Live tracking during the trip, then fare and payment.

How well it must do it

  • Matching in a few seconds.
  • Never give one driver two trips at once.
  • Location updates are many and short lived.
  • Highly available in each city.

Out of scope, say it out loud

  • Pooling, scheduled rides, driver onboarding.

Do the math

5 million drivers online at peak worldwide, each sending location every 4 seconds. 20 million trips a day.

Key point

Location writes dwarf everything else, and only the latest location matters. Keep them in memory, not in a disk database row per update.

The API

driver WebSocket   sends { lat, lng, heading, ts } every 4 s
POST /v1/rides     { pickup, dropoff }       -> ride_id, status searching
driver receives    { type: "offer", ride_id, pickup, expires_in: 15 }
POST /v1/rides/{id}/accept                   (driver)
rider WebSocket    receives driver location and status updates

The data model

Finding nearby drivers with cells

The map is cut into cells using a grid system like geohash, Google S2 or Uber's H3 hexagons. Each driver is stored in the cell they are in. To find drivers near a rider, look in the rider's cell and the cells around it. No scanning every driver in the city.

Watch it work

Press Next or Play. Each step lights up one part and moves the requests. Arrow keys move steps after you click the diagram.

The big picture

  1. Driver connection service receives location updates over persistent connections.
  2. Location index in memory, like Redis with geo commands or a custom service, sharded by city and cell, latest position only.
  3. Matcher per city finds candidates, ranks by time to pickup, offers, and locks drivers atomically.
  4. Trip service with a SQL database for trip state, a state machine from requested to completed.
  5. Pricing reads supply and demand per area from a stream job to compute surge, see lesson 5.2.
  6. Payments at the end, with idempotency keys, see design 10.

Deep dives

Why cells

Searching every driver for distance is too slow. Grid cells turn "near me" into "in these 7 cells", a direct lookup. Hexagons like H3 have neighbours at equal distances, which makes rings of cells even.

Hot path design

1.25 million updates a second go to memory only. A sampled copy goes to a stream for history, safety and ETA models. A lost single update does not matter because the next one arrives in 4 seconds.

No double booking

Mark a driver as offered or busy with an atomic compare and set, only if status is available. Offers time out so a driver who ignores them becomes available again.

Sharding by city

Riders and drivers in Mumbai never match with those in Paris. Sharding everything by city keeps each region small and independent, and a problem in one city stays there.

Data centre failure

Driver apps keep their trip state locally and resend it after a failover, so a trip in progress can be rebuilt even if the server lost recent updates.

Security and abuse

Ride sharing handles location, payment and personal safety.

  • Location history is sensitive. Keep it short, and limit who can see it.
  • Hide phone numbers with masked calling.
  • Detect fake GPS and account sharing.
  • Give riders and drivers safety features, like trip sharing and an emergency button.
