Cost control and FinOps on AWS

In the cloud, every engineer can spend money with one click, so cost becomes everyone's job. Learn how AWS charges, the tools that show where money goes, the biggest levers to cut waste, and the FinOps practice that keeps cost under control as teams grow.

The idea in one minute

Cloud cost is not one big invoice you sign once a year. It is millions of tiny charges, per second of compute, per GB stored, per GB sent, per request. That is great for flexibility, and easy to lose control of.

The main things you pay for are compute, storage, data transfer, especially out to the internet and between regions or AZs, and requests to managed services. Data coming into AWS from the internet is generally free. Data going out usually is not.

FinOps is the practice of managing cloud cost as a team sport between engineering, finance and the business. The FinOps Foundation describes three phases.

  1. Inform. See who spends what, with tags, accounts and reports.
  2. Optimize. Cut waste and buy smarter.
  3. Operate. Make it routine, with budgets, reviews and goals.
Think of it like this

The cloud bill is like a family's electricity bill, where every light and gadget has its own meter. First you label every meter so you know who left the heater on. Then you turn off what nobody uses, buy cheaper bulbs, and check the bill every month together.

Words you need

FinOps
A practice where engineering, finance and business manage cloud cost together.
Cost allocation tag
A tag turned on in billing so costs can be split by it.
Cost Explorer
AWS tool to view and forecast spend by service, account and tag.
AWS Budgets
Alerts when actual or forecast cost or usage passes a limit.
Cost and Usage Report
The most detailed billing data, delivered to S3 for analysis.
Compute Optimizer
Recommends better instance sizes and types from real usage.
Rightsizing
Changing resources to the smallest size that meets the need.
Unit cost
Cost per business unit, such as per order or per active user.
Data transfer out
Data sent from AWS to the internet or other regions, usually charged.

How it works

The biggest levers, in usual order

  1. Turn off what is not used. Idle dev servers, forgotten load balancers, unattached EBS volumes, old snapshots, unused Elastic IPs.
  2. Rightsize. Use Compute Optimizer and real metrics. Try Graviton.
  3. Use the right pricing model. Savings Plans for the steady base, Spot for interruptible work, lesson 4.1.
  4. Store data in the right class with lifecycle rules, lesson 4.2.
  5. Cut data transfer. CloudFront for internet traffic, VPC endpoints instead of NAT gateways for AWS services, keep chatty services in the same AZ when safe.
  6. Use managed and serverless where it lowers total cost, including people's time.

Make cost visible to the people who spend it

  • One account per team and environment, plus required tags, makes ownership clear.
  • Showback shows teams their spend. Chargeback bills it to their budget.
  • Track unit cost, such as cost per 1,000 orders. Total cost going up is fine if orders grew faster.

Cost is an architecture choice

Choices like multi region active active, keeping all logs hot, or chatty microservices across AZs all carry a price. Put cost in design reviews next to reliability and security, as the Well-Architected cost pillar suggests.

Key point

You cannot optimize what you cannot see. Tag and own every resource first, then cut waste, then commit.

Real life scenarios

Dropbox moves storage in house

Dropbox moved most of its storage from AWS to its own data centers around 2016 and reported large savings over the following two years. At very large, steady scale, owning hardware can win. For most companies it does not.

The NAT gateway surprise

A team pulled large container images and S3 data through a NAT gateway every few minutes. Data processing charges became one of their biggest line items. VPC endpoints for S3 and ECR removed most of it.

Cost anomaly alert

An anomaly alert fired on a Sunday. A misconfigured job was writing debug logs at a huge rate. It was stopped after a few hours instead of at the end of the month.

Common mistakes

  • No tags, so nobody owns the spend.
  • Buying Savings Plans before rightsizing.
  • Ignoring data transfer and NAT gateway charges.
  • Looking only at the total bill instead of unit cost.
  • Letting cost be finance's job only.

Security lens

Cost and security are linked. A sudden spike can mean stolen keys used for crypto mining, so budgets and anomaly detection are security alerts too. Deny unused regions with SCPs, limit who can launch expensive instance types, and make sure cost data, which can reveal your architecture, is shared only with the right people.
