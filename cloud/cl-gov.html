Landing zones, Control Tower, compliance and audit

When a company has many teams and many accounts, it needs the same safe starting point for each one and proof for auditors that rules are followed. Learn what a landing zone is, how Control Tower sets one up, the three kinds of controls, and how compliance, data residency and audit evidence work in AWS.

The idea in one minute

Governance means deciding the rules and making sure everyone follows them. In the cloud, good governance is mostly automatic. Rules are written as code and applied to every account.

A landing zone is a ready made, secure multi account setup. It has an organization, a structure of OUs, shared accounts for logs and security, single sign on, networking and guardrails. Every new account "lands" in it with the right settings already in place.

AWS Control Tower builds and runs a landing zone for you. It creates two shared accounts in a Security OU, a Log Archive account and an Audit account, next to the management account. It also gives you an Account Factory to create new accounts with the right settings, and a library of controls.

Controls come in three kinds.

  1. Preventive. Stop a bad action before it happens. Built with SCPs and, in some cases, other policies. Example, "nobody can disable CloudTrail".
  2. Detective. Find bad settings after they happen. Built with AWS Config rules. Example, "flag any bucket without encryption".
  3. Proactive. Check resources before they are created. Built with CloudFormation hooks. Example, "block a template that creates an unencrypted database".
Think of it like this

A landing zone is like a new housing estate that the builder sets up before families move in. Roads, water, street lights, fences and a security office are ready. Every new house is built from the same plan. The rules say no one may remove the smoke alarm, the inspector checks every house each week, and the builder will not approve a house plan without fire exits.

How it works

A common OU layout

  • Security OU. Log Archive and Audit accounts.
  • Infrastructure OU. Shared networking, DNS, shared services.
  • Workloads OU, split into Prod and Non prod, with stricter SCPs on Prod.
  • Sandbox OU. For experiments, with budgets and auto clean up.
  • Suspended OU. For accounts being closed, with an SCP that denies everything.

Compliance in the cloud

  • Compliance is shared. AWS is audited for its part, such as data center security. You must prove your part, such as access control and encryption.
  • AWS Artifact gives you AWS's audit reports, such as SOC 2 and PCI DSS attestations, and agreements such as the HIPAA BAA.
  • AWS Config conformance packs and Security Hub standards map your settings to frameworks like CIS, PCI DSS and NIST.
  • AWS Audit Manager collects evidence from your accounts and maps it to frameworks.
  • Not every AWS service is in scope for every program. Check the services in scope list before you use a service for regulated data.

Data residency

  • Data stays in the region where you store it unless you move or replicate it.
  • Use an SCP that denies other regions, keep backups and replicas in allowed regions, and check where logs and global services send data.
  • For example, India's RBI asked payment system operators in 2018 to store payment data only in India. A Mumbai and Hyderabad design meets that.

Tags, policies and cost governance

Tag policies enforce tag names and values. Budgets per account and OU, and Service Catalog for approved products, keep teams inside the lines without slowing them down.

Key point

Write the rules once as code, apply them to every account automatically, and keep evidence flowing to accounts that workload teams cannot change.

Real life scenarios

Payment data stays in India

In 2018 the Reserve Bank of India directed payment system operators to store payment data only in India. Companies running on AWS used the Mumbai region, and later Hyderabad, with SCPs to block other regions for those workloads.

Hundreds of accounts, one standard

Large companies use Control Tower or their own landing zone code to create hundreds of accounts that all start with the same logging, SSO, network and guardrails, so auditors can check the standard once.

The audit that took days, not months

A fintech mapped its controls to PCI DSS with Config conformance packs and Audit Manager. Evidence was collected all year, so the yearly audit reused it instead of starting from screenshots.

Common mistakes

  • Running workloads in the management account.
  • Letting teams create accounts by hand outside the factory.
  • Assuming AWS's compliance report covers your application.
  • Using a service for regulated data that is not in scope.
  • Forgetting backups and replicas when planning data residency.

Security lens

Treat the management account as the crown jewels. Few people, strong MFA, no workloads. Keep Log Archive and Audit accounts locked down, with separate admins from workload teams. Review SCP exceptions on a schedule. Make break glass access to these accounts logged, alerted and tested.
