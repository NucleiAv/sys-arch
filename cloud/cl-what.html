What the cloud is, and what people use it for

The cloud is computers, storage and networks that you rent over the internet, by the minute, instead of buying and running them yourself. Learn the service models, why companies move to the cloud, and how cloud work splits into infrastructure and architecture.

The idea in one minute

Before the cloud, a company that wanted a website bought servers, put them in a data center, paid for power and cooling, and waited weeks for new hardware. If traffic doubled at Diwali, the site slowed down. If traffic dropped, the servers sat idle but were already paid for.

Cloud computing means you rent these things from a cloud provider, such as Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure or Google Cloud, over the internet. You ask for a server and get it in a minute. You pay only while you use it. When you stop, you stop paying.

The US standards body NIST describes five key traits of cloud computing.

  1. On demand self service. You get resources yourself, with no person to call.
  2. Broad network access. You reach them over the network from anywhere.
  3. Resource pooling. Many customers share the provider's huge pool of hardware.
  4. Rapid elasticity. You can grow or shrink fast, even automatically.
  5. Measured service. Use is measured, so you pay for what you use.
Think of it like this

Owning servers is like owning a car. You pay for it even when it sits in the garage. The cloud is like a taxi. You call one when you need it, pay for the trip, and never worry about oil changes.

Words you need

Cloud provider
A company that rents computing resources over the internet, such as AWS, Microsoft Azure or Google Cloud.
IaaS
Infrastructure as a service. You rent servers, storage and networks and manage the operating system and apps yourself. Example, Amazon EC2.
PaaS
Platform as a service. The provider runs the servers and operating system. You bring your code or data. Example, AWS Elastic Beanstalk, Amazon RDS.
SaaS
Software as a service. A finished app you just use. Example, Gmail, Zoom.
Elasticity
Growing and shrinking resources quickly to match demand.
Pay as you go
Paying only for what you use, often by the second or hour.
CapEx and OpEx
Capital expense, buying hardware up front, versus operating expense, paying as you use.
Public cloud
Resources shared by many customers on the provider's hardware.
Private cloud
Cloud style resources used by one organization only.
Hybrid cloud
A mix of your own data center and a public cloud, connected together.

Cloud as infrastructure, and cloud as architecture

  • Cloud as infrastructure. At its base, the cloud is rented servers, disks and networks. Setting them up, securing them, automating them and running them is infra work. IAM, VPCs, Terraform, CI/CD, monitoring and cost.
  • Cloud as system design building blocks. Providers also sell ready made parts that are exactly the boxes in a design diagram.

  • Cloud as an architecture decision. Regions and zones decide how you design for failure. "Managed or self run" is a trade-off. Pay per use makes cost a design choice. Shared responsibility splits security between you and the provider.

Why companies move, and why some do not

Benefits. Speed, no up front hardware cost, scaling, global reach in minutes, managed services. Concerns. Bills that grow without control, lock in to one provider, data location laws, and new skills to learn.

Key point

The cloud does not remove work. It moves your work from racking servers to choosing services, designing for failure, securing identities and controlling cost.

Real life scenarios

Amazon Web Services starts, 2006

Amazon launched S3 storage and EC2 servers for rent in 2006. Developers could get a server with a credit card in minutes, which changed how startups were built.

Netflix moves to AWS

After a major database corruption in its own data center in 2008 stopped DVD shipments for three days, Netflix moved its streaming service to AWS over about seven years and finished in 2016.

The forgotten test servers

A team started large GPU servers for an experiment and forgot them over a long weekend. The bill was thousands of dollars. Budgets with alerts and auto stop schedules now prevent it.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking the provider secures everything for you.
  • Moving servers to the cloud unchanged and expecting lower cost.
  • No budgets or alerts on the bill.
  • Ignoring where data is stored when laws require a country.
  • Picking a service without checking how it fails.

Security lens

In the cloud, identity is the new perimeter. Anyone with valid keys can reach your resources from anywhere, so stolen access keys are as bad as a stolen data center key. Turn on MFA for the root user and never use it day to day, use roles instead of long lived keys, and keep audit logs from day one. Lesson 2.1 covers this in depth.
