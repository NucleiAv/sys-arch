Step 01

What is a system?

A system is a group of parts that work together to do one job. A restaurant is a system. There is a waiter who takes your order, a kitchen that cooks, a fridge that stores food and a cashier who takes money. Each part has one job. Together they feed you.

Software systems are the same. An app like a food delivery app has parts that take orders, parts that store data, parts that send messages and parts that take payments.

System architecture is the big shape. It names the main parts, the job of each part and how the parts connect. It also holds the big decisions that are hard to change later.

System design is the detailed work inside that shape. It decides how each part does its job, such as the data it stores and the messages it sends.

Think of it like this

If the app is a house, architecture is the floor plan. Two floors, the kitchen next to the dining room. Design is the detail. How many cupboards the kitchen has, how big the pipes are. Lesson 1.1 in Concepts explains the difference fully.

Step 02

Clients ask, servers answer

Almost every app is split into two sides. The client is what you hold, your phone app or your browser. The server is a computer far away that does the heavy work and keeps the data safe.

The client sends a request, like "show me my orders". The server sends back a response, like a list of three orders. That is it. Everything else in system design is about making this simple loop fast, safe and able to handle millions of people at once.

Key point

Why not keep everything on the phone? Because your data must be the same on every device, it must be safe if you lose your phone, and other people need to see some of it, like the restaurant seeing your order.

Request
A message from the client asking for something.
Response
The message the server sends back.
API
The list of requests a server accepts. Like a menu of what you can order.

Step 03

The trip of one request

When you tap "open", your phone first finds the server's address using DNS, the internet's phone book. Then it opens a connection, locks it with encryption, sends the request and waits for the answer.

Every trip across the network takes time. The farther the server, the longer the wait. A trip across the world and back takes about a fifth of a second, even if the server is instant.

Step 04

Latency, throughput and why they are different

Latency is how long one person waits. Throughput is how many people you can serve each second.

Think of a road. Latency is how long it takes one car to drive from A to B. Throughput is how many cars pass per minute. Adding lanes raises throughput, but one car does not get there faster. Building a shorter road lowers latency.

We usually talk about latency with percentiles. p50 is the time that half the requests beat. p99 is the time that 99 out of 100 requests beat. The slowest 1 in 100 matters, because a busy user makes hundreds of requests a day and will hit it often.

Latency
Time for one request. Lower is better.
Throughput
Requests handled per second. Higher is better.
p99
The time that 99 percent of requests are faster than. A measure of the slow tail.

Step 05

When one computer is not enough

One server can handle a few thousand people. What happens when a million show up?

You have two choices. Scale up, also called vertical scaling, means buying a bigger computer. It is simple, but there is a biggest computer, it is very expensive, and if it dies everything dies.

Scale out, also called horizontal scaling, means adding more ordinary computers and spreading the work. A load balancer sits in front and hands each request to one of them. This has no real limit, and if one dies the others carry on. The catch is that the servers must not keep anything important in their own memory. We call that being stateless.

Step 06

Where data lives

Servers come and go. Data must stay. So data lives in special places built to keep it safe.

  • A database keeps the important records, like users and orders. It is careful and correct, but every visit costs time.
  • A cache keeps copies of popular answers in fast memory. It is about 100 times faster than a database, but it is small and can forget.
  • Object storage keeps big files like photos and videos, cheaply.
  • A CDN keeps copies of files in many cities, close to users.
Think of it like this

Your database is the library. The cache is the few books on your desk. Object storage is the warehouse. The CDN is a small branch library in every town.

Step 07

Things will break, plan for it

With one computer, failure is rare. With a thousand computers, something breaks every day. Disks die, networks drop, a bad update goes out.

Good systems expect this. They keep copies of everything important, called redundancy. They notice when a part dies and move the work away from it, called failover. They wait only so long for an answer, called a timeout, and try again carefully, called a retry.

Key point

The goal is not "nothing ever breaks". The goal is "when something breaks, users barely notice".

Redundancy
Having more than one of anything important.
Single point of failure
One part that, if it dies, takes everything down. Hunt these in every design.
Availability
The share of time the system works. 99.9 percent means about 8.8 hours of downtime a year.

Step 08

How an architect thinks

There is no perfect design. Every choice trades one good thing for another. A cache makes reads fast but the copy can be a little old. Many copies of data make it safe but keeping them in sync is hard.

So architects always ask the same questions, in the same order.

  1. What must it do? The features, called functional requirements.
  2. How well must it do it? Speed, size, uptime, called non-functional requirements.
  3. How big is it? Users, requests per second, data per day. Do the rough math.
  4. What are the parts? Draw the boxes and arrows.
  5. Where will it break? Find the busiest and weakest parts and fix them.
  6. What did I trade? Say it out loud.
